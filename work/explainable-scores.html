Case study · Explainable scores

Every AI score should lead back to the calls behind it

The challenge
Hear is an AI platform for contact centers. People coach agents and check compliance from AI scores, so they need to audit them.
What I designed
I designed Compliance, which checks agents against the contact center's rules. I also designed Agent evaluation, which scores each agent. I took it from my first version to V4 + Filter.
Where it stands
Screens from Oct 2026 in Hear Design Lab, a working replica of Hear. Not every version in the Lab is finished.
Role

01 · The problem

An unexplained score leaves two bad options

These scores are about people, and when nothing explains one, the person reading it can only trust the number blindly or ignore it.

Read moreShow less

Agent evaluation started as my first version in the Lab. In the Lab's July 2026 build, it showed scores over time and per skill, with tables of teams and agents. Nothing said why a score was what it was, and Compliance wasn't built yet.

Both screens follow three principles.

  1. Show what matters firstA full grid of agents and criteria where only the failing scores get color.
  2. Explain every numberAny score opens into its place in the team, then into the evidence behind it.
  3. Act from the same screenExplore in Chat, open the agent's evaluation or draft coaching notes, without losing the context.
02 · Compliance

A grid that stays quiet until something fails

Only failing cells get color, so a row of grays looks fine before anyone reads a number.

Read moreShow less

The Compliance home shows each criterion's trend against a dashed target line. The Agents tab holds the grid, with agents across and criteria down. Each cell is one agent's score on one rule over the selected date range.

The last row counts each agent's failing criteria, so the worst columns stand out.

In the light theme, the legend's three passing steps are grays, from dark slate to light gray. Only the two failing steps are coral, and they are exactly the cells the bottom row counts.

Every cell still prints its score, so color is never the only cue. Beside the legend sits a Problems only switch.

03 · The popover

One agent, placed against the whole team

Clicking a cell opens a popover that puts the score in context and says what its main button will do.

Read moreShow less
  • The header names the agent, criterion and date range, so the number is never read without its scope.
  • The score sits next to its difference from the team average, and its rank among all agents.
  • A small histogram shows every agent's score on this criterion, with the team average as a dashed line and this agent's bar lit up.
  • One sentence comes before the buttons: “Opens Chat, which looks up this agent's calls. Nothing extra loads until you click.”
  • Then two ways forward: Explore in Chat as the main button, and Open agent evaluation as a quieter link.
04 · Ask AI

An answer that shows its evidence

Explore in Chat sends the cell to Ask AI, and its answer names the calls that lowered the score.

Read moreShow less

The question arrives already written, with the agent, the criterion and the score in it. The page and the project stay attached as context chips at the top of the chat.

An answer asked from a cell has four parts, and the first three point at something you can check:

  1. What the score is. The average of a stated number of calls, between two stated dates.
  2. Calls that pulled it down most. Each with its call number, its score, the missed step and a short quote from the call.
  3. Pattern. How often the step was missed, and how many other agents miss it too.
  4. Related. Open the lowest-scoring call, compare with the team average or draft coaching notes.

When other agents miss the same step, the answer says the cause may be the script or the process, before it offers Draft coaching notes. A score about one person doesn't automatically become that person's fault.

The chart explains itself too

In Agent evaluation, the score chart's subtitle states its own encoding: height and color are that period's score. Low periods turn coral, like the failing cells in Compliance, and a Poor to Good scale sits under the bars. The controls are written out on the chart: scroll to zoom, drag to select, double-click to reset.

Two ways into the same chat

Ask AI also opens from the top of the sidebar. It says “Ask about what you're looking at” and keeps the page and project as context.

05 · Decisions

Where the design forked

I kept both ways into Ask AI, the cell and the sidebar, and rebuilt the score chart to control its hover.

Read moreShow less
Decision 1 · Starting point

Where should a question about a score start?

  • A blank chatAsk AI from the sidebar. You write the question and say which number you mean.
  • The cell itselfExplore in Chat, the popover's main button, sends the cell's agent, criterion and score with the question.

Result: I kept both. A question from a cell already names its score, so the answer can point to the calls behind it. The sidebar takes open questions about the page.

Decision 2 · The score chart's hover

Keep the first chart, or rebuild it to control what hover shows?

  • Keep the first chartThe area chart from my first version (Recharts). It already drew the score over time.
  • Rebuild it for the hoverIn March 2026 I swapped it for a new chart (ApexCharts), for finer control over the tooltip and markers.

Result: in V4 + Filter, hovering a bar shows the day, the score out of 100 and how many calls it rests on.
