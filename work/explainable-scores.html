Case study · Explainable scores

Every AI score should lead back to the calls behind it

Hear's AI scores contact-center agents, call by call, on the rules they have to follow. People coach agents and check compliance from those scores, so they need to audit them, not just trust them. In Compliance, and in my later versions of Agent evaluation, I designed the path from a score to its evidence: a grid that colors only failures, a popover that places one agent against the team, and an AI answer that names the calls behind the number.

01 · The problem

A score you can't question is a score you can't use

These are scores about people. The person reading them decides who needs coaching, or where a compliance rule is being missed. If an AI produced the number and nothing explains it, there are two bad options: trust it blindly, or ignore it.

The Design Lab is a working copy of Hear that I started, coded with Claude Code from a colleague's starting app. Agent evaluation came with that starting app. In the Lab's July 2026 build it showed a score over time, a score per skill, and tables of teams and agents, but nothing said why a score was what it was. Compliance wasn't built yet.

The screens here are from October 2026: Compliance, and the later Agent evaluation versions I made, V2, V3, V4 and V4 + Filter in its version switcher. The Lab changed a lot that month, and not every version in it is finished.

My job across Hear's product is to make a lot of data feel calm. On these two surfaces that comes down to three moves.

  1. Show what matters firstA full grid of agents and criteria where only the failing scores get color.
  2. Explain every numberAny score opens into its place in the team, then into the calls behind it.
  3. Act from the same screenExplore in Chat, open the agent's evaluation, or draft coaching notes, without losing the context.
02 · Compliance

A grid that stays quiet until something fails

Before the grid, the Compliance home shows each criterion's trend against a dashed target line. The Agents tab is where the detail lives: agents across, criteria down. Each cell is one agent's score on one rule, over the selected date range.

The last row counts each agent's failing criteria, so the worst columns stand out without reading a single cell.

The legend has five steps. In the light theme, the three passing steps are greys, from dark slate to light grey. Only the two failing steps are coral, and they are exactly the cells the bottom row counts.

So a row of greys reads as fine before anyone reads a number, and coral appears only where a rule is failing. Every cell still prints its score, so color is never the only cue. Beside the legend sits a Problems only switch.

Design choice: only problems get color. A full grid of agents and criteria stays quiet until something needs attention.

03 · The popover

One agent, placed against the whole team

Click a cell and a popover opens beside it. It answers three questions in order: how good is this score, compared with whom, and what can I do next.

  • The header names the agent, the criterion and the date range, so the number is never read without its scope.
  • The score sits next to its difference from the team average, and its rank among all agents.
  • A small histogram shows every agent's score on this criterion: the low end, the team average as a dashed line, the high end, and this agent's bar lit up.
  • One sentence comes before the buttons: "Opens Chat, which looks up this agent's calls. Nothing extra loads until you click."
  • Then two ways forward: Explore in Chat as the main button, and Open agent evaluation as a quieter link.

Design choice: the popover says what the next button will do before you press it. Opening cells loads nothing extra; the lookup of calls starts only when someone asks for it.

04 · Ask AI

An answer that shows its evidence

Explore in Chat hands the cell to Ask AI. The question arrives already written, with the agent, the criterion and the score in it. The page and the project stay attached as context chips at the top of the chat.

Every score answer in these screens has the same four parts, and the first three point at something you can check:

  1. What the score is. The average of a stated number of calls, between two stated dates.
  2. Calls that pulled it down most. Each with its call number, its score, the step that was missed, and a short quote from the call.
  3. Pattern. How often the step was missed, and how many other agents miss it too.
  4. Related. Open the lowest-scoring call, compare with the team average, or draft coaching notes.

When other agents miss the same step, the answer says the cause may be the script or the process, before it offers Draft coaching notes. A score about one person doesn't automatically become that person's fault.

Design choice: answers point to the exact calls behind a score, so people can check the AI instead of just trusting it.

The chart explains itself too

The score chart's subtitle states its own encoding: height and color are that period's score, and hovering shows the calls. Low periods turn coral, like the failing cells in Compliance, and a Poor to Good scale sits under the bars. The controls are written out on the chart: scroll to zoom, drag to select, double-click to reset.

Two ways into the same chat

Ask AI also opens from the top of the sidebar. It says "Ask about what you're looking at" and keeps the page and project as context. In this clip, the chart is zoomed into a dip and the question typed is why the score dropped. The answer is general: the usual causes, and where to check. Decision 1 below compares the two ways in.

05 · Decisions

Where the design forked

Two forks on the way from a number to its evidence.

Decision 1 · Starting point

Where should a question about a score start?

  • A blank chatAsk AI from the sidebar, with the page and project as context. You write the question, and you say which number you mean.
  • The cell itselfExplore in Chat, the popover's main button, sends the agent, the criterion and the score along with the question.

Result: both ways in stay in the Lab, and the two clips on this page show the difference. Asked from the sidebar why the chart dipped, the demo answer lists the usual causes and where to check. Asked from a cell, the answer names the calls that pulled that score down, so the AI can be checked instead of just trusted.

Decision 2 · The score chart's hover

Keep the chart that came with the page, or rebuild it to control what hover shows?

  • Keep the imported chartThe area chart from the starting app (Recharts). It already drew the score over time.
  • Rebuild it for the hoverIn March 2026 I swapped it for a new chart (ApexCharts), for finer control over the tooltip and markers.

Result: a custom tooltip with the date, the score in large type and the word "score". In the later V4 + Filter version, hovering a bar shows the day, the score out of 100 and how many calls it is based on.
